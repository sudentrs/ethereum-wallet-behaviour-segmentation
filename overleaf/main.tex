\begin{abstract}
Public Ethereum data make wallet activity observable but do not make wallet populations directly interpretable.
This study examines whether a reproducible behavioural segmentation can be recovered after controlling two common sources of ambiguity: address cohort definition and token-value representation.
Using 2022 ERC-20 transfer activity, we define a year-end code-free cohort of 2,508,997 addresses and cluster a 16-feature count/activity/direction representation that excludes pooled token amounts.
We compare $k=2$--$6$, quantify full-population seed stability and feature-family sensitivity, conduct a corrected-cohort transaction-network null test, and conduct a held-out monthly state analysis using a separate count-only representation.
The three-cluster solution provides a useful working resolution: it has adjusted Rand indices of 0.908--0.971 across three seeds, whereas $k=4$--$6$ are substantially less stable.
Its roles describe sustained activity, distributed lower activity, and short-span concentrated activity.
However, the solution is sensitive to removing persistence/concentration features, so it is not presented as a natural or permanent taxonomy.
In all twelve monthly transaction snapshots, same-state transfer concentration exceeds a shuffled-label null; held-out July--December state persistence also exceeds chance agreement and two-step returns exceed a shuffled future-state null after Holm correction.
The contribution is therefore a controlled and falsifiable workflow for behavioural segmentation, with explicit limits on identity and economic interpretation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Public blockchains expose transaction histories at a scale that is unusual in financial research.
Yet a list of transfers does not directly show how millions of addresses behave as a population.
Wallet segmentation offers one compact description: accounts can be grouped by observable activity patterns rather than by unverified owner identities.
Prior work has used Ethereum activity for address linkage and deanonymization \cite{victor2020address,beres2021blockchain}, supervised behavioural classification \cite{bonifazi2022userspectra}, and temporal graph analysis \cite{agarwal2021temporal,tedge2020}.
These studies show that blockchain behaviour is informative, but an unsupervised partition needs a different standard of evidence.
Every clustering algorithm returns groups, including when a representation is weak, unstable, or accidentally driven by a preprocessing decision \cite{halkidi2001validation,liu2022stability}.

This paper asks: \emph{can a large Ethereum wallet population be organized into behavioural states that remain interpretable under explicit cohort, seed, feature-family, and temporal checks?}
We do not infer real-world identity, ownership, or maliciousness.
We use ``state'' because an address can show different observable behaviour in different months.

The study deliberately narrows the main analysis in two ways.
First, a present-day empty-code check does not establish that an address was an EOA throughout 2022.
We therefore use the more limited, auditable description \emph{year-end code-free}: an address had empty bytecode at the final Ethereum block of 2022.
Second, ERC-20 amounts have token-specific units and are not safely pooled across tokens without an economic normalization.
The main representation consequently excludes all transfer-value features.

Our contribution is a reproducible workflow, not a claim that Ethereum contains one uniquely correct number of permanent wallet types.
It provides (i) a documented year-end code-free cohort and count/activity representation, (ii) a full-population resolution and seed-stability comparison, (iii) feature-family holdouts that expose representation dependence, (iv) a transaction-network shuffled-label test, and (v) a temporally held-out monthly state analysis with frozen preprocessing.

\section{Related Work}
Address-clustering and deanonymization studies use transaction structure to infer links or user profiles \cite{victor2020address,beres2021blockchain}.
Their target is identity or linkage; ours is an identity-agnostic behavioural partition.
Bonifazi et al. represent Ethereum users with behavioural and network time series for predefined-category classification \cite{bonifazi2022userspectra}.
We likewise treat behaviour as temporal, but discover states without category labels.
Temporal graph and embedding studies show that timing and repeated interactions are informative in blockchain systems \cite{agarwal2021temporal,tedge2020}; cryptocurrency networks also evolve over time \cite{liang2018evolutionary}.
Our temporal layer tests state movement rather than predicting a known account class.

Methodologically, the work follows the clustering-validation principle that a partition requires evidence beyond the algorithm output \cite{halkidi2001validation}.
Stability under perturbations is one such check \cite{liu2022stability}.
Here, random seeds and feature-family removals are representation checks; the monthly test is separated from the annual partition because its features and training objective are intentionally different.

\section{Data and Methods}
\subsection{Data and Cohort}
We use monthly ERC-20 transfer records from the ERC-20 Trading 2022 release \cite{somin2025erc2022,somin2025dataset}.
The initial annual address universe contains 2,738,243 addresses.
For each address, we queried Ethereum code at block 16,308,189, the final block of 2022.
Addresses with empty code at that block were retained, yielding 2,508,997 year-end code-free addresses; 229,246 were excluded.
This is a block-scoped operational rule, not evidence that retained addresses were EOAs at every transfer or throughout the year.

\subsection{Annual Count/Activity Representation}
For every retained address, we aggregate ERC-20 transfer activity over 2022 into 16 features: active-day count, activity span and density, total and daily transaction-count summaries, incoming/outgoing/both-active day counts and ratios, directional balance, count coefficient of variation, and transaction concentration.
No pooled token amount, token value, gas, or present-day identity field enters this representation.
Each feature is robust-scaled using its annual median and interquartile range.

We fit k-means for $k=2,3,4,5,6$ with three fixed seeds (492--494), ten initializations per seed, and a maximum of 200 iterations.
For every pair of seeds we report adjusted Rand index (ARI), normalized mutual information (NMI), and Hungarian-matched agreement \cite{hubert1985partitions,vinh2010information,kuhn1955hungarian}.
Silhouette is computed on a fixed 5,000-wallet sample and is descriptive rather than a proof of the correct $k$ \cite{rousseeuw1987silhouettes}.

For the selected working resolution, we remove three prespecified feature families in turn: activity level, persistence/concentration, and directionality.
The resulting partitions are compared with the full-feature K3 labels.
These are sensitivity tests, not external validation.

\subsection{Held-Out Monthly State Analysis}
The annual clusters must not be reinterpreted as monthly labels.
Instead, we construct one count-only feature vector for each active wallet-month: transactions per calendar day, active-day fraction, transactions per active day, daily-count coefficient of variation, peak-count share, and span fraction.
We sample active wallet-months from January--June to fit one RobustScaler and a three-state MiniBatchKMeans model.
Those transformations and centroids are frozen before assigning July--December states.
An absent wallet-month is missing, not a behavioural state.

For each held-out adjacent pair, we compare observed self-transition probability with chance agreement from the marginal state frequencies and report Cohen's $\kappa$ \cite{cohen1960agreement}.
For four held-out triples, we restrict to wallets that changed in the first step and test whether returning to the initial state exceeds a shuffled future-state null with 999 permutations.
We apply Holm correction across the four return tests \cite{holm1979sequential}.

\subsection{Transaction-Network Null Test}
We separately test whether annual K3 labels align with ERC-20 transfer interactions.
For each month, we retain transfer records whose sender and recipient both belong to the year-end code-free cohort, then draw a uniform 200,000-edge sample.
The observed statistic is the same-label edge fraction.
The null preserves sampled edges and randomly permutes annual K3 labels among the unique endpoint wallets 999 times.
Thus, the test asks whether same-state transfer concentration exceeds the level expected after breaking the association between wallet identity and its K3 label while retaining the sampled network and label composition.
It is a network-homophily test, not a causal test and not a community-detection evaluation.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Resolution and Seed Stability}
Figure~\ref{fig:resolution} shows that compactness and stability do not select the same resolution.
K2 has the largest sampled silhouette (0.357) but yields a highly dominant cluster (72.4--83.2\% across seeds), making it a coarse summary.
K3 has a lower silhouette (0.298) but strong agreement across all seed pairs: ARI 0.908--0.971, NMI 0.853--0.944, and Hungarian agreement 0.973--0.992.
K4--K6 are materially less stable, particularly by ARI (0.268--0.729 across their seed pairs).
We therefore use K3 as a working resolution for interpretation, not as an estimate of a uniquely true number of roles.

\subsection{K3 Behavioural Profiles and Sensitivity}
The reference K3 run assigns 22.8\% of the cohort to a \emph{sustained-activity} state, 69.6\% to a \emph{distributed lower-activity} state, and 7.6\% to a \emph{short-span concentrated-activity} state.
The sustained state has median 29 active days, 73 transactions, and a 268-day span.
The distributed lower-activity state has medians of 9 active days, 16 transactions, and a 173-day span.
The short-span state also has median 16 transactions, but only 4 active days and a 7-day span; its median activity density is 0.75 and its transaction-concentration ratio is 0.50.
These labels describe observed activity patterns, not user categories or identities.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/k3_count_activity_profiles.pdf}
\caption{Selected annual K3 median profiles. The vertical scale is $\log(1+\text{median})$ so count and ratio features can be displayed together; the underlying numerical profiles are reported in the replication results.}
\label{fig:profiles}
\end{figure}

Feature-family removals qualify this interpretation.
Removing activity-level features gives ARI 0.591 and matched agreement 0.868 versus the full K3 labels; removing directionality gives ARI 0.574 and agreement 0.860.
Removing persistence/concentration features gives ARI 0.350 and agreement 0.617.
Thus K3 is reproducible under initialization but meaningfully dependent on the joint representation, especially temporal persistence and concentration features.

\subsection{Transaction-Network Alignment}
The corrected network analysis supports an association between annual K3 labels and transfer endpoints.
Across all twelve months, the observed same-state transaction fraction exceeds the shuffled-label null at the 999-permutation resolution (raw $p=0.001$ in every month; Holm-adjusted $p=0.012$ across the twelve monthly tests).
The observed fraction ranges from 0.502 to 0.603 (mean 0.559), compared with null means from 0.415 to 0.482 (mean 0.468).
The mean observed-minus-null difference is 0.092.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/k3_network_observed_vs_null.png}
\caption{Monthly annual-K3 same-state transaction concentration against the shuffled-label null. Each month uses 200,000 sampled transfers with two cohort endpoints and 999 label permutations.}
\label{fig:network}
\end{figure}

Coverage is limited: only 13.7\%--19.3\% of raw monthly ERC-20 transfers have two endpoints in the year-end code-free cohort and can enter this test.
The result therefore concerns the retained within-cohort interaction subgraph rather than the full ERC-20 network.
It supports label--network alignment under this null, but cannot distinguish direct same-state preference from shared applications, degree effects, or other unmodelled mechanisms.

\subsection{Held-Out Monthly States}
The monthly model is deliberately independent of the annual labels.
Across July--December adjacent pairs, 571,659--786,661 wallets are active in both months.
Observed persistence ranges from 0.572 to 0.611, compared with chance agreement from 0.426 to 0.450; $\kappa$ ranges from 0.253 to 0.293.
The pooled held-out transition matrix in Fig.~\ref{fig:monthly} shows that all three monthly states have substantial, but not complete, self-transition probability.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\columnwidth]{figures/k3_heldout_transition_matrix.pdf}
\caption{Pooled July--December transition probabilities for the frozen three-state monthly model. These state IDs are not annual K3 role IDs.}
\label{fig:monthly}
\end{figure}

Among wallets that switched state at the first step, return to the starting state after a second month is consistently above the shuffled future-state null: observed return is 0.417--0.447 versus null means 0.391--0.419.
All four tests have raw permutation $p=0.001$ and Holm-adjusted $p=0.004$.
This supports structured, partly reversible monthly state movement, conditional on a wallet being active in all months required by the test.

\section{Discussion}
The primary conclusion is deliberately narrower than a claim of a universal Ethereum taxonomy.
After year-end cohort control and removal of pooled token-value features, the count/activity representation yields a reproducible three-state working resolution.
Its seed stability is strong, but its feature-family sensitivity demonstrates that the labels summarize a particular behavioural representation rather than intrinsic wallet identities.

The network and temporal evidence strengthen the state interpretation in different ways.
Within the observed cohort subgraph, annual K3 labels align with transfer endpoints more than shuffled labels do.
Active wallets also show above-chance persistence and above-null two-step return in held-out months.
It does not show that an address remains in an annual K3 role over time, because annual and monthly representations answer different questions.
It also does not model inactive months as a state, explain why transitions occur, or establish causal effects of market events.

We intentionally do not carry forward the previous pooled-value role claims, present-day EOA claims, or K4--K6 network results into this version.
The corrected K3 network result is limited to the sampled within-cohort subgraph and does not establish a mechanism for the observed alignment.
Additional work should audit historical code status at transfer-time or monthly block boundaries, compare token-aware economic representations, repeat the analysis across years and chains, and externally validate any application-specific labels.

\section{Conclusion}
This study presents a controlled workflow for Ethereum wallet segmentation rather than treating a k-means output as a final result.
For 2,508,997 year-end code-free addresses, a count/activity-only K3 representation provides a stable and interpretable working partition, while finer K4--K6 alternatives are unstable across seeds.
Feature-family holdouts show that this conclusion depends especially on persistence/concentration information.
A corrected network null test finds above-null same-state transfer concentration in every monthly snapshot, and a separate frozen monthly model finds above-chance persistence and two-step return in held-out data.
Together, these results support the cautious claim that large Ethereum populations can be summarized as reproducible behavioural states under a documented representation, not as fixed real-world identities.
